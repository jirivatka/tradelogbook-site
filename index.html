Alpaca does not tell you what a trade made

The API returns orders and fills, not results. A buy and a sell come back as two unrelated rows, and pairing them is left to you. Logbook does that pairing on your device — first in, first out, per symbol, using Alpaca’s own position_intent so a short closed lower reads correctly as a win.

Every row shows what the trade rode on and what it returned, because −90,76 means nothing until you know it was staked on roughly 24 700 — which makes it a scratch, not a disaster.

The orders that never filled

This is the part no other client shows. An order can be submitted, accepted, and then expire without ever trading — and if it was the exit on an open position, that position is still open. On a strategy meant to be flat overnight, that is a real loss hiding in a place nobody looks.

This is not hypothetical. Logbook’s first run against a real paper book surfaced an expired sell from three weeks earlier. Reconstructing it turned up a 77-share position that had carried for three days on an intraday strategy — and a P&L record that was wrong by $356.90.

Fees included, because they change the answer

Alpaca charges no commission on equities, which is not the same as free. It passes on SEC, FINRA and CAT fees, billed per day, and they appear on an endpoint the orders API never mentions. A total computed from orders alone is always slightly flattering.

Logbook fetches them, itemises each charge under the day it belongs to, and nets the total — so the figure it shows reconciles with your account balance to the cent.

Where the money came from, not just what the trades did

A deposit moves your equity and appears in no trade history anywhere — so the number changes and nothing explains it. Logbook reads your account’s cash activity too: money you paid in or took out shows in the timeline alongside your trades, and it is never counted as a trading result, because you put it there rather than earning it.

Dividends and interest are the opposite case. They are earned, and a closed-trade figure structurally cannot show them, so they are totalled on their own line instead of being folded into your trading result or left out of it altogether.

And a balance that adds up

Put those together and the app can do the one check that tests the whole rather than the parts: what you paid in, plus what you made, plus income, less the fees, plus what your open positions are worth — against the equity Alpaca reports independently. If the two agree, it says so. If they do not, it says that instead, and by how much.

Small differences are expected and named rather than hidden: every result is worked out from the exact quantity and price, while the money that actually moved was rounded to the cent on each fill.

On iPad, your accounts stay in view

Accounts sit in a sidebar and the book fills the rest, so moving between a paper account and a live one does not cost you the one you were reading. Rows keep a readable width instead of stretching across a 13-inch display, and every number and label follows your Larger Text setting.

Read-only, by construction

Logbook cannot place, modify or cancel an order. Not as a setting — the code that would do it does not exist. It is a viewer for accounts that are traded by something else, whether that is you or a strategy you run.

Your keys stay yours

API keys are verified once against Alpaca and then stored in your device’s Keychain. There is no Logbook account, no server, and no analytics in the app. Nothing you connect is transmitted anywhere except to Alpaca’s own API, under your own credentials.

Before you start

  • An iPhone or iPad running iOS 17 or later. The unlock is a single purchase tied to your Apple Account, so buying it on one covers the other.
  • An Alpaca brokerage account, paper or live, and an API key pair from your Alpaca dashboard.
  • Prices come from Alpaca under your own data plan. On the free plan that is the IEX feed — a fraction of consolidated volume, not the full tape.
  • Alpaca allows one market-data stream per account. Logbook polls over REST instead, so it will not take the connection your own bots are using.
